Abstract

Road crashes killed an estimated 1.19 million people worldwide in 2021 {{cite:who2023}}, and Kenya records over four thousand road deaths a year, most of them pedestrians and motorcyclists {{cite:ntsa2023}}. Many of these crashes begin with a driver who looked away at the wrong moment: at a phone used for navigation, or at a hazard noticed too late. Driver-assistance technology that addresses this, such as augmented-reality (AR) head-up displays and automatic emergency braking, is sold almost exclusively as factory equipment in new, expensive vehicles, and none of it can be fitted to the older cars that make up most of Kenya’s fleet.

This project proposes a Virtualized AR Windscreen Display with Semi-Autonomous Driving Support System: a retrofit platform that fuses a 2K camera, a 360° LiDAR, satellite positioning, an inertial sensor and the car’s own OBD-II data into a real-time model of the road ahead. The model drives two things. First, a precision AR navigation display, in which the route is drawn onto the live camera view locked to the measured road surface and to the correct lane, rather than as a generic arrow floating over the image. Second, a semi-autonomous safety layer that detects and tracks road users, warns of hazards and reckless driving, operates the indicators and hazard lights, and, as a last resort, applies a force-limited brake through a fail-safe actuator which the driver can always override.

The system is split between a laptop with an NVIDIA RTX 5060 GPU, which runs four TensorRT neural networks (object segmentation, Kenyan road-sign detection, lane detection and depth), sensor fusion, tracking, offline routing for a 100 km region around Nairobi and GPU rendering; and an STM32 microcontroller hub running FreeRTOS, built on two custom circuit boards: a windscreen pod behind the mirror carrying the camera and inertial sensor, and an under-dash power box whose outputs all default to “off” in hardware. The hub owns every actuator, reads the car’s own CAN bus, and enforces the safety rules independently of the laptop, including release within 200 ms if the laptop stops responding. Much of the software has already been built and tested against recorded road video and a recorded Nairobi drive: the four networks run together in a median 17.8 ms per frame against a 33 ms budget, the AR renderer runs at 54–82 frames per second at 2560×1440, and the braking logic fires at the intended time-to-collision in simulation. The remaining work is hardware integration, calibration, a mandatory actuator bench test, and staged validation from a bench, to a stationary car, to an empty field, to a closed campus road at 5–10 km/h. The estimated hardware budget is KES 195,000–270,000, most of which is the LiDAR.
