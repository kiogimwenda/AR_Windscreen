1.0 Introduction and Background

Driving is mostly a visual task. A driver continuously reads the road ahead, the vehicles and people around them, the signs, and, increasingly, a navigation screen mounted on the dashboard or held in the hand. Every glance away from the road is a period in which the driver is effectively blind to what happens in front of the car, and naturalistic driving studies have repeatedly found that such glances are among the strongest predictors of a crash {{cite:klauer2006,dingus2016}}.

Two families of technology have grown up to address this. The first is the head-up display (HUD), which places information in the driver’s line of sight so that the eyes do not leave the road. Its most advanced form, the augmented-reality (AR) HUD, goes further: it draws graphics that appear attached to the real world, such as a navigation path lying on the correct lane or a highlight around a pedestrian, so that the driver does not need to translate a map or an abstract symbol into the scene in front of them {{cite:gabbard2014,bark2014}}. The second family is advanced driver-assistance systems (ADAS): forward collision warning, lane keeping, and autonomous emergency braking (AEB), which use cameras and radar to detect danger and, in the case of AEB, brake the car when the driver does not. Large studies of AEB have found it reduces rear-end crashes substantially {{cite:cicchino2017}}.

Both families share a limitation that matters in Kenya: they are factory-fitted to new, mid-range and premium vehicles and cannot be added afterwards. Commercial AR windscreen products, such as those demonstrated by WayRay and by vehicle manufacturers, are proprietary, costly and tied to specific car models. Most cars on Kenyan roads are older imports that will never receive them. At the same time, the components from which such a system is built (cameras, compact LiDAR sensors, satellite receivers, inertial sensors, consumer GPUs that can run neural networks in real time, and inexpensive microcontrollers) have become affordable and widely available.

This project proposes to combine those components into a retrofit platform: a Virtualized AR Windscreen Display with Semi-Autonomous Driving Support System. It is “virtualized” because, instead of an optical combiner projected on the glass, the first version shows the AR view on a screen as a live camera image with the graphics composited into it (video see-through AR), while the software is structured so that an optical display can replace the screen later without changing anything else. It is “semi-autonomous” because the car never drives itself: the system informs the driver, operates the indicators, hazard lights and horn, and, only when a collision is imminent and the driver has not reacted, applies a limited brake that the driver can always overpower.

The central idea is that a single, precise, real-time model of the road ahead serves both purposes. The same sensor fusion that tells the navigation display exactly where the road surface and the lane are also tells the safety layer where every vehicle, cyclist and pedestrian is, how fast they are moving and where they are likely to go next.

1.1 Problem Statement

Kenya’s National Transport and Safety Authority recorded 4,690 road deaths in 2022, of whom 1,682 were pedestrians and 1,254 were motorcyclists {{cite:ntsa2023}}. Globally the World Health Organization estimates 1.19 million deaths in 2021, with more than nine in ten occurring in low- and middle-income countries {{cite:who2023}}. Three driver-side problems contribute to this toll and are the focus of this project:

  1. Delayed recognition of hazards. Pedestrians crossing between vehicles, motorcycles filtering through traffic, vehicles braking suddenly and vehicles driven recklessly are recognised late, especially at night, in rain and in dense urban traffic. The time the driver has left to react is often shorter than their reaction time.
  2. Distraction caused by navigation. Drivers who do not know the route glance repeatedly at a phone or a dashboard screen and must mentally map a two-dimensional arrow onto a complex junction. Each glance removes their eyes from the road; the 100-car naturalistic study found that glances away from the forward roadway of more than two seconds roughly doubled near-crash and crash risk {{cite:klauer2006}}. Kenyan roads add a further difficulty: road works, closures and new roads change the network faster than a static map can follow.
  3. No automatic protective response. In the vehicles most Kenyans drive, nothing reacts on the driver’s behalf when a collision becomes imminent: no warning, no hazard lights to alert following traffic, no emergency braking.

Solutions to each problem exist, but only as factory-fitted, model-specific and expensive equipment. There is therefore no affordable, retrofittable system that gives the driver of an ordinary car precise, eyes-on-the-road navigation and hazard awareness, together with a safe, bounded automatic response when the driver does not react in time.

1.2 Problem Justification

Solving this problem is worthwhile for four reasons.

The need is large and local. The groups most at risk on Kenyan roads, pedestrians and motorcyclists, are exactly those a camera–LiDAR perception system can detect and highlight. Even a modest reduction in late recognition of such road users would have real value.

The evidence supports the approach. AR navigation that places guidance on the road reduces the need to look away, and drivers using it have reported clear benefits over a conventional display {{cite:bark2014}}, provided the graphics are designed not to become a distraction themselves {{cite:kim2022}}. Forward collision warning combined with automatic braking has been associated with a large reduction in front-to-rear crashes with injuries {{cite:cicchino2017}}.

A retrofit is now technically and economically feasible. A consumer laptop GPU can run several neural networks on high-resolution video in real time, a compact LiDAR costs a fraction of earlier automotive units, and open data and software (OpenStreetMap, OSRM, TensorRT, FreeRTOS) remove licensing costs. The whole system is estimated at KES 195,000–270,000, far below the cost of a vehicle equipped with comparable factory features.

It is a substantial engineering project in the department’s core areas. It spans embedded systems and real-time firmware, power and actuator electronics, communication protocols, digital signal processing and estimation, computer vision and machine learning, computer graphics, and safety engineering, which is the breadth expected of a final year project in Electronic and Computer Engineering.

1.3 Objectives

1.3.1 Main Objective

To design, implement and test a precision, sensor-fused augmented-reality navigation and driver support system, whose real-time model of the road also drives hazard detection and fail-safe, driver-overridable vehicle actuation, as a retrofit for an ordinary car.

1.3.2 Specific Objectives

  1. To design and implement a real-time perception and sensor-fusion pipeline that detects, segments and ranges vehicles, pedestrians, cyclists, lanes and Kenyan road signs from camera and LiDAR data, and estimates the car’s own motion from GNSS, IMU and OBD-II, processing 2K video at 30 frames per second (all neural networks within a 33 ms frame budget) with a road-sign detection accuracy of at least 0.70 mAP50.
  2. To design and implement a road-locked AR navigation display that routes offline across a 100 km region around Nairobi, matches the car’s position to the road, and draws the route in the correct lane on the measured road surface at 24 frames per second or more, with the drawn path within 2 pixels of the calibrated camera model and visibly inside the correct lane within LiDAR range.
  3. To design, implement and validate a hazard-assessment and fail-safe actuation subsystem that warns of hazards and reckless driving, operates indicators, hazard lights and horn, and brakes through a force-limited actuator only when time-to-collision falls below 1.8 s and the driver has not braked, verified by passing all six actuator bench checks on three consecutive attempts (including driver override, release within 200 ms of link loss, and release when the power box is disconnected) before staged in-vehicle testing at 5–10 km/h.

1.4 Scope and Limitations

In scope. The full sensing, perception, fusion, tracking, navigation, decision and rendering software on the laptop; the sensor and actuator hub (firmware, and two custom PCBs with their enclosures: a windscreen pod and an under-dash power box); a roof mount for the LiDAR; a force-limited brake actuator with a fail-safe release; a physical emergency-stop switch; a bench rig for the actuator; installation in one test vehicle; and staged validation up to a closed campus road section at 5–10 km/h.

Out of scope for this project, and presented as future work: an optical combiner or projector on the windscreen (the software’s display interface is designed so this can be added without other changes); steering intervention of any kind; fully autonomous driving; thermal cameras; and cloud connectivity or mobile applications.

Limitations. Navigation precision is highest within LiDAR range, where the road surface is measured, and falls back to a flat-road assumption beyond it; this will be reported as it is. Testing is limited to low speeds on closed or empty ground, so stopping-distance calibration at road speeds is not claimed. A live road-status layer (traffic and incidents) depends on a commercial data provider’s licence terms and is deferred; the system routes on an offline OpenStreetMap graph that is refreshed regularly and corrected by the car’s own sensors.
