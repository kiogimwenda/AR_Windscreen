3.0 Methodology

This chapter describes how the system will be built and verified: the development approach (3.1), the overall architecture (3.2), the hardware and firmware of the hub (3.3–3.4), each software subsystem on the laptop (3.5–3.9), calibration (3.10), testing and staged validation (3.11), and how the work will be demonstrated (3.12). Design calculations are given where a component or threshold is chosen.

3.1 Development Approach

The project is developed in seventeen phases (0–16), each with written exit criteria that must be demonstrably met before the next phase begins ({{ref:fig_methodology.svg}}). Each phase starts with the concept and the interface, then the implementation, then tests, including tests designed to fail if the code is wrong (for example, a projection test that fails if lens distortion is left out). Software that does not need hardware is developed and verified first against recorded road video, a recorded GNSS drive and synthetic scenes, so that when the hardware arrives, only integration remains. Every task is recorded in a progress log (what was done, why, and how it was verified), and every choice made under ambiguity is recorded in a decision log, which together form the evidence base for the final report.

Hardware work follows a strict order with a mandatory gate: no part of the system may act on a real vehicle until the brake actuator has passed its bench test (Section 3.11). All physical work (wiring, mounting, testing in the car) is carried out by the student.

3.2 System Architecture

The system has four layers ({{ref:fig_architecture.svg}}). Sensors observe the road and the car. A sensor and actuator hub, an STM32F405 microcontroller running FreeRTOS, reads the low-rate sensors (GNSS, IMU, OBD-II over the car’s CAN bus, gesture), owns every actuator, and enforces the safety rules. The host, a laptop with an NVIDIA RTX 5060 GPU running Debian Linux under WSL2, reads the high-rate sensors (camera over USB, LiDAR over Ethernet) and runs perception, fusion, navigation, decision-making and rendering. The outputs are the AR display, the signal relays, the brake actuator, and an evidence log.

The split follows one principle: the laptop decides, the hub disposes. The laptop has the computing power for vision and mapping but runs a general-purpose operating system that can crash or stall. The hub is simple, deterministic and always running. The laptop may only send a bounded request; the hub decides whether to act on it, clamps it to its own limits, and releases the brake by itself if the laptop falls silent for 200 ms.

{{fig:fig_architecture.svg|System architecture, from sensors to outputs|5.0}}

Physical layout ({{ref:fig_installation.svg}}). The hub is built as two units on custom printed circuit boards. A windscreen pod behind the rear-view mirror holds the camera, the microcontroller, the IMU and the GNSS receiver. An under-dash power box holds everything that switches current: the actuator driver, the relays, the kill relay and the connection to the car’s OBD-II port. A 25-way cable joins them. The LiDAR sits on the roof, tilted forward, and the emergency stop and gesture sensor are within the driver’s reach.

{{fig:fig_installation.svg|Installation layout, and the geometry of the tilted LiDAR mount|3.4}}

Inside the host, each subsystem runs in its own thread and communicates through lock-free single-producer, single-consumer ring buffers carrying FlatBuffers messages ({{ref:fig_software_dataflow.svg}}, Appendix D). A consumer always takes the newest message, so a slow stage (for example, the renderer during a heavy frame) never makes another stage fall behind. The decision arbiter is the only component permitted to produce an actuation request, which makes the safety-relevant path short and auditable.

3.3 Hardware Design: the Two-Box Hub

Why two boxes. The camera belongs behind the mirror, where the wipers keep its view clean and it sees roughly what the driver sees. Placing the IMU on the same rigid carrier plate as the camera fixes the camera–IMU geometry and lets the IMU measure the camera’s own vibration, which the renderer can use to hold overlays steady over bumps. The actuator driver, relays and OBD connection, by contrast, belong under the dash beside the pedal, fuse box and OBD port, where heavy currents stay short and away from the sensitive receivers ({{ref:fig_hub_hardware.svg}}).

Pod board (4-layer). An STM32F405RGT6 (Cortex-M4F, 168 MHz) was chosen for its CAN controllers and hand-solderable LQFP64 package. An 8 MHz crystal is mandatory: USB needs a 48 MHz clock within ±0.25 %, while the internal RC oscillator is only about 1 % accurate. The IMU (BNO085) and GNSS receiver (u-blox NEO-M8N) are mounted as breakout boards on standoffs in the first version. The pod takes 5 V from the power box or from the laptop’s USB port through a diode-OR, so it stays running if either cable is pulled, and can report what happened.

Power board (2-layer, 2 oz copper). The rule that makes the split safe is that every line from the pod enters the power board through a pull-down resistor. If the microcontroller is in reset, unpowered, or the cable is unplugged, the brake drive, the cable magnet and every relay read “off” in hardware, with no firmware involved. A 74HCT244 buffer converts the 3.3 V signals for the 5 V module inputs (its TTL input threshold of 2.0 V reads 3.3 V reliably). The 12 V input is protected by fuses, reverse-polarity protection and a TVS diode against load-dump transients, feeding a 60 V-rated buck converter.

Actuation power path. The actuator is fed through a fuse and the contacts of a 40 A automotive relay whose coil is powered through the normally-closed contact of a mushroom-head emergency stop. Pressing it, a broken wire or a lost supply all remove actuator power in hardware. A BTS7960 H-bridge drives the actuator with 20 kHz PWM, above the audible range, and an ACS712-20A Hall-effect sensor measures its current independently.

OBD-II over CAN. The hub reads vehicle speed and engine rpm directly from the car’s CAN bus using the ISO 15765-4 diagnostic protocol {{cite:iso15765}}, replacing a Bluetooth ELM327 adapter. Used vehicles now imported into Kenya must have been first registered in 2019 or later, and CAN has been the norm for OBD-II since about 2008. The transceiver sits in the power box beside the OBD port, so the hub adds only a short unterminated branch to the car’s bus; a 2.5 m branch would cause reflections at 500 kbit/s. The firmware never disturbs the bus. It listens silently before transmitting at each candidate bit rate, sends each read-only request only once, and polls at most about 20 times a second.

Fail-safe actuator design. A lead-screw linear actuator is rejected because it cannot be back-driven: it would hold the brake on after a power cut, and typical units are also too strong (up to 900 N) and too slow (5–15 mm/s). The design uses (i) a pull-only cable to the pedal, which goes slack if the driver presses further, so the driver’s foot always overrides it; (ii) a holding electromagnet in the cable, powered through the kill relay, which drops the cable free whenever power is lost, and whose holding force is a mechanical force ceiling; and (iii) a fast, low-force actuator of about 50–100 mm/s and 100–200 N. It is subject to supervisor approval before purchase.

{{fig:fig_hub_hardware.svg|The two-box hub: windscreen pod, inter-box cable and under-dash power box|4.6}}

LiDAR mounting. The manufacturer forbids anything, glass included, in the sensor’s field of view {{cite:livox2024}}. The unit is also rated only to 55 °C, so it cannot sit behind the windscreen. Its vertical field of view runs from −7° to +52°, so mounted level at h = 1.55 m its lowest beam meets the road only at

d = h / tan 7° = 1.55 / 0.123 = 12.6 m(3.1)

Tilted 15° forward on a 3 mm aluminium wedge plate, which also serves as the heatsink the manual asks for, the forward lower edge becomes −22°. The road is then visible from 1.55/tan 22° ≈ 3.8 m, or from wherever the bonnet stops blocking the view (typically 4–5 m). That covers the 5.0 m gap at which the 10 km/h brake decision is made (Section 3.8). Only the rearward view is lost, and nothing uses it.

Current sensing. The ACS712 outputs 2.5 V at 0 A and 100 mV/A on a 5 V supply. It is divided by 2/3 inside the power box, so that nothing above 3.3 V travels up the cable:

VADC = (2.5 + 0.1 I) × 2/3  ⇒  at the 8 A limit, VADC = 2.20 V ⇒ 2,730 of 4,095 counts(3.2)

One count is 12 mA of actuator current. An unplugged sense line reads about 0 V, which this formula would turn into 25 A, so the firmware ignores the reading whenever the power box is absent. Otherwise, every cable unplug would latch a false overcurrent fault.

Enclosures and power. The pod is printed in ASA or PETG, since PLA softens at about 55 °C and the glass zone gets hotter in the sun. It clips onto a bracket bonded to the glass in the uncoated area near the mirror, and it has a matte hood to stop dashboard reflections reaching the lens. The laptop draws 200–280 W under GPU load, more than a USB-C car charger supplies, so it runs from a 300 W inverter or on its own battery during the short test windows.

3.4 Host–Hub Protocol and the Safety Core

Framing. Every message is framed as 0xAA | type | length (2 bytes, little-endian) | payload | CRC-16/CCITT-FALSE (2 bytes). Four message types are used: a 63-byte SensorReport from hub to host at 50 Hz; a 9-byte ActuationCommand (indicator and light bits, brake intensity 0–255, duration, host timestamp); an 8-byte AckStatus reporting what the hub actually applied and any fault; and an empty Heartbeat the host must send at least 10 times a second. The payload structures are compiled on both sides with static size checks, so a layout mismatch becomes a build error rather than silently wrong data. The SensorReport stream needs (63 + 6) × 50 = 3.45 kB/s, a negligible load for full-speed USB. The parser re-synchronises on the start byte after any corrupt frame; if more than 10 of the last 50 frames are corrupt, actuation is disarmed until 20 clean frames arrive.

Tasks. The firmware has four FreeRTOS tasks: a sensor task that assembles the 50 Hz report; a comms task that parses and sends frames; an actuation task that applies commands every 10 ms; and a watchdog task that checks the link every 20 ms. All safety decisions are made by a single, hardware-independent SafetyCore module, which is unit-tested on the laptop.

Safety state machine ({{ref:fig_hub_states.svg}}, Appendix C). The hub boots disarmed with the actuator released before any task runs. It arms only while it receives fresh heartbeats, its own report loop is alive, the emergency stop is released, the power box is connected and powered, and the frame-error rate is acceptable. Any failure of these returns it to disarmed, releasing the brake and forgetting the last command. A brake command is clamped to an intensity of 90 (of 255) and a duration of 1.5 s regardless of what the host asks for. An actuator current above 8 A for 30 ms latches a fault that only a power cycle clears. The watchdog task releases the actuator directly, without waiting on any shared lock, and the IWDG hardware timer resets the whole microcontroller if the firmware itself hangs for 500 ms.

The brake force is therefore limited in four independent places: the host arbiter’s configured intensity (60 of a maximum 90), the hub’s compiled ceiling of 90, the driver’s own clamp in the actuator driver, and the electromagnet’s holding force.

3.5 Perception

Camera pipeline. A 2K (2560×1440) USB camera with manually locked focus supplies 30 frames per second. Autofocus is disabled because every focus change would invalidate the camera calibration. Each frame is timestamped at capture and shared, not copied, with all consumers.

Neural networks. Four networks are exported to ONNX and compiled into TensorRT FP16 engines for the RTX 5060 ({{ref:models}}). Pre-processing (letterboxing, normalisation) runs on the GPU in CUDA, and each network’s output is decoded on the host into boxes, masks, lane points or a depth map ({{ref:fig_frame_pipeline.svg}}, Appendix D).

3.6 Sensor Fusion, Tracking and Motion Prediction

Ego-motion (EKF). The car’s own state x = [px, py, ψ, v, ω] (position in a local metric frame, heading, speed, yaw rate) is estimated by a 5-state extended Kalman filter {{cite:thrun2005}}. It predicts at the IMU rate with the constant turn rate and velocity (CTRV) model

px′ = px + (v/ω)[sin(ψ + ωΔt) − sinψ],   py′ = py + (v/ω)[cosψ − cos(ψ + ωΔt)],   ψ′ = ψ + ωΔt(3.3)

(with the straight-line limit when ω → 0) and corrects with GNSS positions and OBD-II wheel speed using the standard update K = PHT(HPHT + R)−1, x ← x + K(z − h(x)). GNSS latitude and longitude are converted to the local frame with the WGS-84 meridian and prime-vertical radii of curvature.

Mask-based camera–LiDAR fusion. The Livox Mid-360 produces about 200,000 points per second over 360°×59°. For each camera frame the fusion step (i) moves every LiDAR point to where it would have been seen at the camera’s timestamp, using the ego speed and yaw rate (without this, a 100 ms scan at 10 m/s smears objects by 1 m); (ii) removes points within 0.15 m of the fitted road plane; (iii) projects the rest into the image with the calibrated lens model; (iv) keeps only the nearest point per small image cell, since the LiDAR can see points the camera cannot; (v) assigns points to an object only if they fall inside its eroded mask; and (vi) takes the object’s range from the nearest depth cluster holding at least 20% of its points, never the mean, so a few background points cannot move a pedestrian several metres. Objects with fewer than three points receive a MiDaS-based estimate scaled against LiDAR-ranged objects in the same frame, marked estimated and never used for braking. LiDAR clusters in the car’s path that no detection explains become unknown obstacles: the camera can add information but can never veto what the LiDAR physically measures.

Tracking. Each object is tracked by an IMM filter {{cite:blom1988}} mixing constant velocity, CTRV and stopping models. Detections are associated with tracks by the Hungarian algorithm {{cite:kuhn1955}} within gates derived from each track’s innovation covariance {{cite:barshalom2001}}. A track is confirmed after repeated detections and new tracks must pass a per-class maximum-speed check, which prevents false associations from creating implausible tracks.

Motion prediction. For every track the predictor produces a position distribution up to 2 s ahead (drawn in the AR view as a ribbon whose width shows uncertainty), the closest point of approach to the ego car, and a Monte-Carlo collision probability. With relative position r and relative velocity v:

t* = −(r·v)/|v|2,   d* = |r + vt*|(3.4)

Unlike time to collision, the closest point of approach also captures crossing pedestrians and vehicles cutting in. Predictions feed warnings only; they never trigger the brake (Section 3.8).

3.7 Precision Navigation

The navigation pipeline answers three questions: which road to take, where the car is on it, and where that road is in the camera image ({{ref:fig_navigation.svg}}, Appendix D).

Routing. OSRM {{cite:luxen2011}} runs locally with the multi-level Dijkstra algorithm on an OpenStreetMap extract covering everything within 100 km of Nairobi’s CBD (as far as Thika, Machakos, Naivasha and beyond). A script downloads the daily Kenya extract, verifies its checksum, clips it, builds the routing data, and only switches to the new map after a smoke-test route from the CBD to Thika succeeds; any failure leaves the previous map in service. The Nairobi CBD to Thika route is 43.7 km. MLD was chosen because it allows road weights to be changed in seconds {{cite:delling2011}}: when a closure is detected, the route is recomputed in about 1.2 s.

Road status in three layers. The map is treated as an expectation, and the sensors as the truth. Layer 1 is the regularly refreshed OpenStreetMap base. Layer 2, a live traffic and incident feed from a commercial provider, is designed but deferred until its licence terms permit this use. Layer 3 is the car’s own observations (a closed road, a blocked lane), which always override the map.

Map matching. The fused pose, not raw GNSS, is matched to the road network with the HMM method of Newson and Krumm {{cite:newson2009}}, using a sliding trace of recent positions. The matcher reports which road the car is on and its distance along the route. It is throttled to about 10 Hz and resets its trace after a gap of more than 10 s. On a recorded 2,773-fix drive in Nairobi, the median distance from fix to matched road was 3.1 m and 98.7% of fixes were within 20 m; the remaining fixes were a genuine deviation from the planned route, which the matcher correctly reported as off-route.

Road-surface projector. This is the step that makes the route appear painted on the road. From the matched position it samples the route every 1.5 m out to 60 m ahead and gives each point a height: the mean of measured LiDAR ground points within 1 m where available; the fitted road plane where the road is hidden (for example by a car ahead); and flat ground beyond LiDAR range. Two corrections come from the detected lane lines. A heading correction fixes compass error, which matters because a 3° heading error displaces a point 60 m ahead by 60 × tan 3° = 3.1 m, a whole lane. A lateral correction moves the line from the road’s centre line (which is what OpenStreetMap records) into the car’s lane. Both are persistent estimates that a single frame can change only by a bounded step, so one bad lane detection cannot throw the line across the road; frames are used only when both lane lines are found and the lane is 2.3–5 m wide.

3.8 Hazard Assessment and the Decision Arbiter

Hazard assessment. A rule-based classifier flags, for each confirmed track: tailgating (the ego car’s time gap to the vehicle ahead below 1.0 s); erratic speed (speed variation left after removing a steady trend); swerving (lateral wander after removing a steady turn); sudden braking (a high IMM stopping-model probability); crossing (a closest-approach conflict with high collision probability); forward collision risk; and a vulnerable road user in the path. These combine into a risk value r ∈ [0, 1] that sets the colour and motion of the hazard overlay. Rules were chosen over a learned classifier because each flag can be explained and tested.

Decision arbiter ({{ref:fig_arbiter.svg}}). Four rules are evaluated in priority order every cycle, and the first that matches wins:

  1. Brake when the time to collision with a confirmed object in the ego path, whose range was measured by the LiDAR within the last 200 ms, is below 1.8 s, the driver is not already braking, and the system is armed (fresh hub report, emergency stop released, no actuator fault, and the evidence log able to record);
  2. Hazard lights when the car itself decelerates harder than 0.4 g;
  3. Warning (overlay and tone only) for a high or critical hazard in the path;
  4. otherwise, a zeroed request that releases everything.

Only rule 1 can brake, and it uses only the current measured gap and closing speed:

TTC = gap / closing speed,   closing speed > 0.5 m/s(3.5)

Braking on predictions or probabilities would risk phantom braking, which is itself a hazard. Every request, and the release that follows it, is written to the evidence log with its full context before it is sent.

{{fig:fig_arbiter.svg|Decision arbiter rules, in priority order|4.3}}

Is 1.8 s enough at test speeds? At the 10 km/h upper limit of the campus tests (v = 2.78 m/s), braking starts when the gap is 2.78 × 1.8 = 5.0 m. The expected latency from capture to pedal movement is about 0.6 s (one frame of 33 ms, about 20 ms of inference, fusion and arbitration, under 20 ms of USB and hub cycle time, and up to 0.4 s for a 50–100 mm/s actuator to take up about 40 mm of pedal travel), during which the car covers 2.78 × 0.6 = 1.7 m. At a moderate deceleration of 0.3 g (2.94 m/s²) the stopping distance is

s = v2/2a = 2.782/(2 × 2.94) = 1.3 m(3.6)

so the car stops within about 3.0 m of the trigger point, leaving about 2 m of margin. The threshold is a conservative starting value to be tuned during testing, not a final figure; its behaviour was verified in simulation, where an approaching object first triggered braking at a true TTC of 1.70 s, inside the 1.2–1.9 s acceptance window.

3.9 AR Rendering and Display

The renderer composes the camera image and all overlays on the GPU with OpenGL 4.6. Under WSL2 this runs through Mesa’s Direct3D 12 translation on the NVIDIA GPU; the renderer checks at start-up which GPU it received and refuses a software fallback. Each frame, the video is uploaded as a texture and the overlays are drawn in one pass from a compact overlay scene produced by the other subsystems. The main overlay types are:

  • the route band, drawn in 3D on the projected road surface with perspective-correct interpolation, solid where the surface is measured and fainter in the far field;
  • hazard glows following each object’s mask, coloured and animated by risk level, with predicted-path ribbons, a following-distance zone for tailgating, and a “barrier” for a stop sign;
  • sign badges (for example, speed-limit grading) and turn instructions.

Overlays never hide a hazard: route graphics are occluded by objects in front of them using each object’s measured depth. The 3D overlays are moved by the car’s own motion between the moment their pose was computed and the moment the frame is shown, which compensates for processing latency. The display is behind a DisplaySink interface; the only sink built now is a window on the laptop screen, and an optical projector sink can be added later without changing the rest of the system.

Measured at 2560×1440 on real road video with a realistic overlay load, the renderer ran at 54–82 frames per second (target 24 or more), with the overlay pass taking about 2 ms and the video upload 7–10 ms; the shader’s projection agreed with the calibrated camera model to within 2 pixels ({{ref:ar_render_krakow.jpg}}).

3.10 Calibration

Camera intrinsics (focal lengths, principal point and the five distortion coefficients k1, k2, p1, p2, k3) are estimated with Zhang’s checkerboard method {{cite:zhang2000}}, with focus locked. Extrinsics relate the camera, the LiDAR and the vehicle frame (x forward, y left, z up, origin on the ground below the rear axle). The LiDAR–camera transform is estimated from a calibration target and then checked at every start-up by measuring how well LiDAR points fall inside detected object masks; a falling score over time signals that a mount has moved. A repeatable road-projection check, driving slowly along a marked road and confirming the route stays in the lane without floating or sinking, follows every recalibration.

IMU mounting. The IMU is tilted with the camera, so its readings are rotated into the vehicle frame before fusion. Read raw at a 12° pitch, the measured yaw rate would be 2 % low and would pick up a fifth of the body’s roll rate in corners. The rotation is calibrated from data the hub already reports. Roll and pitch come from gravity, averaged while the car is at rest on level ground (roll = atan2(ay, az), pitch = atan2(−ax, √(ay² + az²))). Yaw comes from straight-line acceleration and braking, with the sign taken from OBD speed. At every stop, the angle between measured and expected gravity reveals a knocked pod.

3.11 Testing and Staged Validation

Software tests. Every module has unit tests (GoogleTest on the host; Unity for the hub’s protocol and safety logic, which is compiled to run natively on the laptop), run with address and undefined-behaviour sanitizers. Safety-critical code is also tested by fuzzing: 200,000 random inputs to the decision arbiter must never produce a brake above the ceiling, and 600,000 random events, including cable unplugs, must never leave the hub’s actuator applied in an unsafe state. Tests are themselves checked by mutation: deliberately breaking a safety rule must make a test fail. Integration tests replay recorded video and drives.

Actuator bench gate. Before any in-vehicle work, the actuator is mounted with a real used brake pedal on a bench rig with a load cell, and must pass six checks on three consecutive attempts: (1) the rig check, confirming the actuator and pedal are on the bench and not in a vehicle; (2) a sweep across brake intensities, logging the intensity the hub actually applied (evidence that its ceiling holds whatever is asked), peak current, peak force and response time; (3) a person pushes the pedal back by foot while the actuator holds its maximum intensity, the most important check in the project; (4) the emergency stop is pressed mid-actuation and the drive and current must fall to zero; and (5) the USB cable is physically unplugged mid-actuation and the actuator must release, and its current fall to zero, within 200 ms with no software command. The hub measures (5) itself and reports it once the cable is reconnected. In check (6), the inter-box cable is pulled mid-actuation: the actuator must go limp at once, and the hub must report the fault without latching a false overcurrent. Only after three consecutive passes is the maximum brake intensity treated as final. The whole checklist is then repeated on the custom PCB hardware, since new hardware needs its own evidence.

Staged validation then proceeds in four stages, each with a written checklist (Appendix E): A, the bench gate above; B, stationary in the vehicle, with the handbrake on and wheels chocked; C, an empty field with a spotter, at low speed; and D, a formally closed campus road section at 5–10 km/h, with spotters, an agreed abort signal, and a manual confirm-to-brake step on the first runs before automatic braking is allowed.

3.12 Demonstration and Presentation Plan

The presentation will follow the same staged-risk order as the testing: it opens with video of the bench test, showing a foot overriding the actuator and the watchdog release, so the safety story is established before anything else. It then shows the AR display live on the stationary car, with a person walking into view to trigger detection, highlighting and (where the venue allows) a visible, overridable actuation. Moving tests are shown as recorded video rather than attempted live. Results are presented as measured figures (frame rate, inference time, TTC accuracy, actuator response time, navigation lateral error) drawn directly from the evidence log, and the talk closes with the future optical display, which the DisplaySink interface is designed to accept.
