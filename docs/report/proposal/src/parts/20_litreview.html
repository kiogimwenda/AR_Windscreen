2.0 Literature Review

This chapter reviews the work on which each part of the proposed system builds: why driver attention and navigation matter (2.1); augmented-reality head-up displays (2.2); deep-learning perception from a camera (2.3); fusing the camera with LiDAR and estimating motion (2.4); routing and map matching (2.5); and collision avoidance and functional safety (2.6). Each section ends by stating what this project takes from the literature and where it differs. Section 2.7 draws the gaps together.

2.1 Road Safety, Driver Attention and Navigation Displays

The WHO’s 2023 global status report estimates 1.19 million road deaths in 2021, a slight fall from 2010 but still the leading cause of death for people aged 5–29, with more than 90% of deaths in low- and middle-income countries and more than half among pedestrians, cyclists and motorcyclists {{cite:who2023}}. Kenyan figures follow the same pattern: pedestrians and motorcyclists together made up more than 60% of the 4,690 deaths recorded in 2022 {{cite:ntsa2023}}. Any system meant for Kenyan roads must therefore detect vulnerable road users reliably, not only other cars.

The link between inattention and crashes is well established by naturalistic driving studies, in which ordinary drivers’ cars are instrumented with cameras and sensors for months. Klauer et al. analysed the 100-car study and found that any glance away from the forward roadway lasting more than two seconds at least doubled the risk of a crash or near-crash, and that secondary tasks were a factor in a large share of events {{cite:klauer2006}}. Dingus et al., using the much larger SHRP 2 dataset of over 900 crashes, found that drivers were engaged in some observable distracting activity during more than half of their driving time, and that handling a hand-held phone raised crash risk several-fold {{cite:dingus2016}}.

These studies show why a conventional navigation display is itself part of the problem: to use it, the driver must look down, read an abstract map, and translate it back to the scene. Placing guidance in the driver’s line of sight and attaching it to the road is the obvious response, and leads to head-up and AR displays.

2.2 Augmented-Reality Head-Up Displays

Gabbard, Fitch and Kim reviewed the prospects for automotive AR and set out both its promise and its difficulties {{cite:gabbard2014}}. The promise is that graphics registered to the world (appearing to lie at the correct place in the scene) remove the mental translation step: a turn arrow painted on the correct lane needs no interpretation. The difficulties are technical and human. Registration must be accurate and stable, or the graphics drift, jitter or appear to float, which is worse than no graphics at all. The graphics must not hide real hazards. And the system’s latency must be low enough that the overlay keeps up with the car’s motion.

Bark et al. built “Personal Navi”, a navigation aid on a see-through 3D volumetric HUD, and compared AR guidance that appeared at the depth of the road with conventional guidance in a driving study {{cite:bark2014}}. The study reported advantages for guidance presented at the depth of the road, supporting the idea that guidance attached to the road reduces the effort of following a route. Their system, however, relied on a specialised optical display and on a pre-defined route geometry, not on sensing the road itself.

The risk of AR adding distraction rather than removing it is addressed by Kim and Gabbard, who measured how different AR HUD designs affect drivers’ visual attention {{cite:kim2022}}. They found that the distraction potential depends strongly on how the graphics are designed and placed, and that it can and should be measured rather than assumed. This project turns that finding into design rules: one consistent visual language, graphics that stay on the road surface, graphics shown only when relevant, and no overlay covering a hazard.

Commercial systems follow the optical route. Production AR HUDs from vehicle manufacturers, and holographic windscreen displays shown by companies such as WayRay, project graphics through the windscreen with an optical combiner. They are engineered for a specific car’s windscreen and dashboard, are proprietary, and are not available as retrofits.

Relevance to this project. The proposal adopts the AR principle and the design rules from {{cite:gabbard2014,kim2022}}, but makes two different choices. First, it uses video see-through AR on a screen for the first version, which removes the optical engineering while keeping the registration problem, the hard part. Second, the registration is driven by measurement: the route is placed using the LiDAR-measured road surface and the detected lane lines, rather than a flat-road assumption or pre-defined geometry.

2.3 Camera-Based Perception with Deep Learning

Object detection and segmentation. Redmon et al. introduced YOLO, which treats detection as a single regression from the image to boxes and class probabilities and so runs in real time, unlike earlier two-stage detectors {{cite:redmon2016}}. The family has since gone through many versions; Terven, Córdova-Esparza and Romero-González review them up to YOLOv8, whose anchor-free head and improved training give a good balance of speed and accuracy {{cite:terven2023}}. Bolya et al. showed with YOLACT that instance segmentation, a pixel-accurate mask for each object, can also run in real time, by predicting a small set of prototype masks per image and a set of coefficients per object {{cite:bolya2019}}. YOLOv8’s segmentation variant uses the same idea. Masks matter for this project for two reasons: the AR highlight can follow a pedestrian’s outline, and, more importantly, the mask selects exactly which LiDAR points belong to the object (Section 2.4).

Lane detection. Qin, Wang and Li reformulated lane detection as selecting, for each of a set of predefined image rows, which column the lane passes through, rather than classifying every pixel {{cite:qin2020}}. This “row anchor” approach is far faster than segmentation and copes with lanes hidden by vehicles because it reasons about the whole image. Their follow-up, UFLDv2, adds column anchors and an ordinal classification loss, improving accuracy on curved and side lanes while keeping the speed {{cite:qin2024}}.

Monocular depth. Ranftl et al. trained MiDaS on a mixture of datasets with a scale- and shift-invariant loss, producing a network that predicts relative depth for almost any image {{cite:ranftl2022}}. Its output has no metric scale, so it cannot on its own give a distance in metres, but it is useful for ordering objects and, when scaled against a few measured points, for estimating the range of objects the LiDAR does not see.

Traffic signs. Ertler et al. released the Mapillary Traffic Sign Dataset (MTSD), with over 100,000 street-level images from around the world and more than 300 sign classes {{cite:ertler2020}}. Its global coverage makes it a better starting point for Kenyan roads than datasets collected in one European country, since Kenyan signs follow the Vienna-convention style with local variations.

Relevance to this project. Detection alone gives what and where in the image, not how far. Most published systems evaluate one network at a time on a desktop GPU. This project runs four networks together (YOLOv8m-seg, a YOLOv8 sign detector trained on MTSD and audited on Kenyan images, UFLDv2 and MiDaS) within one frame period on a laptop GPU, and uses each network’s output as an input to geometry and fusion rather than as an end result.

2.4 Camera–LiDAR Fusion, State Estimation and Tracking

Geometry and calibration. Fusing a camera with any 3D sensor starts from the pinhole camera model and its lens distortion, which map a 3D point to a pixel {{cite:hartley2004}}. Zhang’s method estimates these parameters from several views of a flat checkerboard and is the standard procedure for camera calibration {{cite:zhang2000}}. The rigid transform between LiDAR and camera (the extrinsics) must also be known; small errors in it move projected points off the objects they belong to.

Fusion. Qi et al.’s Frustum PointNets use a 2D detection to cut out the frustum of 3D points behind each box and then find the object inside it with a learned network {{cite:qi2018}}. Vora et al.’s PointPainting goes the other way: it projects every LiDAR point into a semantic segmentation of the image and “paints” it with the class scores before 3D detection {{cite:vora2020}}. Both show that the camera is best at deciding what something is and the LiDAR at measuring where it is. Both, however, rely on trained 3D networks and on large labelled LiDAR datasets. Box-based selection also has a known weakness: a pedestrian’s bounding box is mostly background, so points from the wall behind them contaminate the range.

State estimation. The Kalman filter {{cite:kalman1960}} is the optimal estimator for linear systems with Gaussian noise; the extended Kalman filter (EKF) linearises a nonlinear model around the current estimate, and the unscented filter of Julier and Uhlmann propagates sample points instead, which handles stronger nonlinearity {{cite:julier2004}}. Thrun, Burgard and Fox give the probabilistic framework, including motion models for vehicles and the fusion of satellite, inertial and odometry measurements {{cite:thrun2005}}.

Tracking manoeuvring targets. A single motion model cannot describe a car that cruises, turns and brakes. Blom and Bar-Shalom’s interacting multiple model (IMM) filter runs several models in parallel (for example constant velocity, constant turn rate, and stopping) and mixes them according to how well each explains recent measurements {{cite:blom1988}}. Tracking many objects adds the data association problem, deciding which detection belongs to which track, which is commonly solved as an assignment problem with the Hungarian algorithm {{cite:kuhn1955}} inside validation gates {{cite:barshalom2001}}.

Relevance to this project. The proposal fuses with instance masks rather than boxes: only points inside an eroded mask are assigned to an object, and the range is taken from the nearest dominant cluster of those points, never their mean. This needs no trained 3D network and removes background contamination at its source. Tracking uses an IMM filter with constant velocity, constant turn rate and velocity (CTRV) and stopping models, which also provides a per-object probability that a vehicle ahead is braking.

2.5 Routing and Map Matching

Luxen and Vetter presented OSRM, an open-source routing engine for OpenStreetMap data that answers continental-scale route queries in milliseconds by preprocessing the road graph {{cite:luxen2011}}. Delling et al.’s customizable route planning separates that preprocessing into a metric-independent partition of the graph and a fast customisation step that applies road weights {{cite:delling2011}}. OSRM’s multi-level Dijkstra (MLD) mode implements this idea, which means that closures or traffic can re-weight the graph in seconds without rebuilding it.

Raw GNSS positions are typically in error by several metres and cannot say which road, let alone which lane, the car is on. Newson and Krumm formulated map matching as a hidden Markov model: candidate road positions near each fix are hidden states, emission probabilities fall with distance from the fix, and transition probabilities favour routes whose driven length matches the straight-line distance between fixes; the Viterbi algorithm then finds the most likely path {{cite:newson2009}}. This is robust to noise and to sparse fixes and is the basis of OSRM’s own matching service.

Relevance to this project. Routing and map matching establish which road and how far along it the car is, but not where the road is in the camera image with lane-level precision. The proposal therefore adds a step not found in these works: a projector that places the matched route on the LiDAR-measured road surface and corrects its heading and lateral position from the detected lane lines.

2.6 Collision Avoidance and Functional Safety

Lee showed that drivers regulate braking using time to collision (TTC), the current gap divided by the closing speed, which can be perceived directly from how fast an object’s image grows {{cite:lee1976}}. TTC has since become the standard trigger quantity in forward collision warning and AEB. Cicchino’s analysis of police-reported crashes in the United States found that vehicles with forward collision warning plus low-speed AEB had about half the rate of front-to-rear crashes of the same models without them {{cite:cicchino2017}}.

A system that can apply the brakes is safety-critical. ISO 26262 sets out the functional-safety lifecycle for road-vehicle electronics: hazard analysis, safety goals, a safe state for every fault, independence between the function and its monitor, and verification evidence at each stage {{cite:iso26262}}. Two concerns stand out for a retrofit. The first is that a false activation, phantom braking, is itself a hazard. The second is that software on a general-purpose computer cannot be trusted to guarantee a safe state.

Relevance to this project. The proposal does not claim ISO 26262 compliance, which is beyond a student project, but adopts its principles: a defined safe state (brake released), an independent monitor (the hub, which enforces limits and releases on its own watchdog), a hardware kill path (the emergency stop), and an evidence log of every decision. To limit phantom braking, braking is triggered only by the measured TTC of a confirmed, LiDAR-ranged object, never by a prediction or a learned model, as recommended by the TTC literature {{cite:lee1976}}.

In summary, the improvements this project makes over existing work are: (i) measured registration of the AR route, from the combination of LiDAR ground model, lane detection and map matching; (ii) mask-based fusion that gives a reliable range to each detected object without training a 3D network; (iii) one road model serving both navigation and safety; and (iv) a retrofit, fail-safe actuation path in which an independent microcontroller, a hardware kill switch and a mechanical force limit each keep the brake safe even if the laptop fails.
